Survivor-informed participatory reflection

Better is not the same as safe

Migrant and refugee girls who are still in protective care in Trinidad and Tobago, and girls who have left it: what helps, what hurts, and what they want to change.

Neera Abigail Carrizales Ramroop November 2026 18 former residents 6 current residents

Prepared for the Youth and Survivor Summit, Manila, Philippines, 2026. Based on an anonymous survey with 18 former residents and a focus group with 6 current residents. To protect participants, the centre, staff and girls are not named.

executive summary

What the girls told us

Girls still in care and girls who have left agree on what hurts most, and both told us what must change. Leaving does not end the risk: better is not the same as safe.

This reflection listened equally to two groups of migrant and refugee girls connected to a protective care centre in Trinidad and Tobago: six girls who are still living there (focus group) and eighteen who have left, most now back in Venezuela (anonymous survey). Both groups used the same lists, so their answers can be compared. Together they describe what helps, what hurts, and what they want to improve, for themselves and for the girls who come after them.

Why this matters for ending violence against children

  • The cycle starts with not knowing. Poverty, family breakdown and lack of schooling push girls to leave, often without knowing the risks. Girls did not know entering irregularly was a crime, that people traffic children, or even that Trinidad is a different country. A passport costs more than families can pay.
  • Protection itself can cause harm. Not knowing what will happen, a short family call every two weeks, rationed mental health support, rules that feel like punishment and public humiliation are harms too. In one girl's words, leaders must "saber ayudar y no perjudicar" (know how to help without causing harm).
  • Protection stops at the exit. Half of former residents had no follow-up, girls who turn 18 are "released", and girls inside fear being taken again by the same people. Without support that continues across the border, the cycle can restart.

Six main findings

1

Not knowing and too little family contact hurt most

The hardest parts of care for former residents (11 and 9 of 18), and all 6 current residents agreed with both.

2

Healing has to be for every girl

Memories of the past are the heaviest load inside (ranked first by 4 of 6). Psychological support, the most valued help for former residents (13 of 18), is now offered only to some. Girls ask for adults who listen, advise and keep it private.

3

Education is their hope

Schooling, English and trade skills are the tools girls see for a legal, independent future. Losing access to education is their top worry about returning (5 of 6).

4

Freedom also means how girls live inside

Excessive rules applied unequally, rules designed for other residents, public humiliation, the language barrier and an imposed culture make care feel like punishment.

5

Better is not the same as safe

Former residents still face risks (11 of 16) and half had no follow-up. Girls inside fear family rejection, losing their schooling and being trafficked again.

6

The cycle starts with not knowing

Girls left without information about trafficking, irregular status, language or even geography.

Key recommendations

  • Psychological support for every girl, and training in psychological first aid and trauma-informed care for the adults who look after them.
  • Family contact at least weekly, and regular, clear information about each girl's case.
  • House rules reviewed with the girls and applied equally, with small everyday freedoms and respectful discipline.
  • An individual safety assessment before every return, a transition plan before 18, and follow-up for 6–12 months across borders.
  • School re-entry and catch-up programmes for girls who return.
  • Honest information in Venezuela about trafficking and migration risks, and lower barriers to legal migration.
context

What happens after they leave is largely unknown

Programme and policy decisions rest on what is seen inside the centre, not on girls' lives afterwards.

The displacement crisis in Venezuela has brought many children and adolescents to Trinidad and Tobago, some unaccompanied and some separated from family. Girls who enter the immigration system can be detained or placed in protective accommodation while their cases are resolved. They are later reunited with family, returned to Venezuela, placed elsewhere, or released when they turn 18.

The girls arrive after journeys shaped by poverty, family separation, interrupted schooling, gender-based violence, exclusion and limited access to services. In care they receive counselling, education, English classes, vocational training and daily support. Public attention, such as Amnesty International's 2020 report and media coverage of child deportations, tells the story about these children. Girls themselves are rarely asked what helped, what was missing, and what would have prevented harm, whether they are still in care or have already left.

purpose

The questions we asked

Girls still in careWhat makes life in care harder or easier, what worries them about leaving, and what would they change so that care protects them and prepares them for what comes next?

Girls who have leftAre they returning to the same conditions that placed them at risk, and what support do they believe would have been most meaningful in preventing those outcomes?

Six vulnerabilities frame the reflection: poverty and economic insecurity, family separation or instability, lack of education, gender-based violence, social exclusion, and limited access to services.

Objectives

  1. Listen to girls still in care and girls who have left as equal experts on what protection feels like.
  2. Understand, from both groups, what helps, what is hard and what is missing during care.
  3. Learn what girls still in care worry about for the future and what they would change now.
  4. For girls who have left, assess whether support in care lasted and compare their vulnerabilities before and now.
  5. Compare how the two groups see the same supports, difficulties and worries.
  6. Bring both groups' recommendations to programme and policy discussions on ending violence against children.
methodology

A survivor-informed participatory reflection

A small, mixed-methods inquiry that treats girls as experts on their own lives. It is not a formal evaluation of the centre and does not claim statistical representativeness. "Reflection" signals that it describes patterns in girls' own accounts to inform practice and policy.

Survivor-informedGirls' views shape the questions, and nobody is asked to retell experiences of harm.
ParticipatoryCurrent residents check and add to the former residents' findings, and their messages to leaders are shared in their own words.
Mixed methodsCounts from closed questions, plus reflexive thematic analysis of open answers.
LanguageAll tools for girls in simple Spanish; quotes kept in Spanish with English translations.

The survey was shared twice, which explains the higher number of responses; there are no duplicates. It asks about life now, time in the centre, leaving, a before/now grid covering the six vulnerabilities, and what would have helped (Annex A).

The focus group used the same three answer lists, so the groups can be compared directly (Annex B). Girls found choosing only three options hard and asked to rank each list from most to least important instead. A traffic-light round asked whether five of the former residents' findings were true for girls inside now, and the session closed with what they would tell their past selves. The two facilitators recorded a debrief straight after.

Ethics and safeguarding

  • Consent and assent: former residents confirmed their age; those under 18 confirmed guardian agreement, and anyone who declined was taken to the end. For current residents, consent came from whoever holds legal responsibility for them in care, plus each girl's own assent. Seeing the questions before deciding helped hesitant girls feel safe to take part.
  • Anonymity: no names, ages or other identifying details were collected. Findings are reported as group counts and unattributed quotes.
  • No effect on services: participants were told that taking part or not would not affect their case, stay or support.
  • Sensitive topics: pregnancy, violence and absconding were not asked about directly. Leaving "on my own" was offered as an option without the word "escape".
  • Focus group: questions were framed around "girls" in general, and nobody was asked to tell her own story. Names in the debrief transcript were removed during analysis.
  • Safeguarding follow-up: concerns the girls raised (fear of being trafficked again, breaches of confidentiality, public humiliation) are shared with the relevant services as general feedback, without identifying anyone.

Analysis

  • Closed questions: reported as counts ("11 of 18", "4 of 6"), not percentages, because the samples are small.
  • Ranked votes: for each option, how many of the 6 girls ranked it and how many ranked it first.
  • Before/now grid: for each vulnerability, how many girls marked it before the centre and how many marked it now.
  • Open answers, focus group notes and debrief: reflexive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke.
  • Comparison: where former and current residents agree, the finding is strongest; where they differ, the difference is reported as a finding.
limitations

The girls we could not reach matter most

  • Who was reached: the survey travelled through WhatsApp contacts. Girls who kept in touch and were doing well enough to answer are likely over-represented, so the real picture after leaving may be harder than reported.
  • Relationship with the researcher: the researcher worked closely with the former residents, and many named her with affection; all 18 rated the form 5 out of 5. This trust made the responses possible, but it probably tilted answers toward the positive. None of the current residents knew her from that time.
  • Who facilitated the focus group: two Spanish-speaking members of the Venezuelan community who visit the girls regularly, including with faith-based support. Familiarity helped girls speak openly, but may have made prayer and community visits more likely to come up.
  • Small numbers, one centre: 18 survey responses and 6 focus group participants from one centre describe patterns, not proof.
  • Group dynamics: one girl tended to speak first and others built on or disagreed with her. Conflicts with other girls were not discussed at all, and would need one-to-one conversations.
  • No adult perspective: frontline workers and the principal did not take part, so the reflection presents girls' views alone and is not an evaluation of the centre.
  • The before/now grid: almost no girl ticked both columns for the same situation, which probably reflects how the form was read. Before and now counts are reported side by side.
  • No contact details or disclosure questions: former residents live in another country, beyond the researchers' reach to offer follow-up support. The tools therefore did not ask for contact details or for information about harm that would make the researchers ethically responsible for acting when they could not. This protects the girls, but it means no individual follow-up is possible, including for the one former resident who feels "not safe at all".

4. Before and now

Most vulnerabilities fell after the centre, but lack of money stayed the same and lack of services and documents rose.

Patterns across questions (small numbers, read with care):

  • Aging out: the two girls marking the most vulnerabilities now (five and six, including violence) both turned 18 and were released.
  • Return without support: all 3 girls who say life is the same or harder went back to Venezuela, 2 to family and 1 to a temporary centre.

5. Themes from girls' own words

Six themes came through the open answers (preliminary reflexive thematic analysis).

Family is the strongest protection

Family contact was the second-hardest part of care, the most common missing support, and the most common thing that would have helped after leaving (5 girls).

Waiting is a harm in itself

Uncertainty about their case was the hardest part of care and follows girls after they leave.

Dignity, not criminalisation

Girls asked again and again to be listened to without judgement.

People make the difference

Adults who listened were remembered with gratitude, and losing one could undo a girl's sense of safety.

Arrival and keeping busy

The first weeks were the hardest; activities and courses helped girls "distraer la mente" (occupy the mind).

Pregnancy and early motherhood

Although not asked, girls raised a lack of support during pregnancy in care; 3 now care for a child.

"Es inhumano que una semana llamen unas y las otras lloren de desesperación porque debían esperar dos semanas más por llamar para saber de su familia, 5 minutos."

It is inhumane that one week some call while others cry in desperation because they have to wait two more weeks to hear from their family, 5 minutes.

"Es demasiado difícil enfocarte en un nuevo comienzo si aún hay cosas de tu pasado persiguiéndote como si eso nunca fuese a acabar."

It is too hard to focus on a new beginning when things from your past are still chasing you, as if it will never end.

"Nos veían solo como delincuentes que entraron sin permiso a su país, cuando en realidad solo éramos unas niñas frustradas, engañadas, confundidas y dolidas."

They saw us only as delinquents who entered their country without permission, when really we were just girls who were frustrated, deceived, confused and hurt.

"Al principio me sentía bien hasta que se fue la traductora… desde el momento que ella se fue mi mundo volvió a estar solo."

At first I felt fine, until the interpreter left… from the moment she left my world was alone again.

to world leaders

"Detrás de cada chica migrante hay una niña con sentimientos, sueños, miedos y una familia que extraña… Les pediría que no solo se preocupen por nuestra seguridad, sino también por nuestro bienestar emocional."

Behind every migrant girl there is a little girl with feelings, dreams, fears and a family she misses… I would ask them to care not only about our safety, but also about our emotional wellbeing.

Healing for every girl

Psychological support is now offered only to girls judged to have a "big trauma". Some had never seen a psychologist. Without their old escapes, girls face their memories without the skills to manage them; they agreed it feels like rehab.

Listen, advise, keep it private

Girls repeated "me escuchan y me aconsejan" and feel calm and less alone afterwards. Some staff share what girls tell them, and some girls feel pushed to tell their story.

Education is hope

Schooling, English and trade skills are tools to support themselves legally and recover lost years. Losing schooling is their top worry about going back, because families need them to work.

Freedom inside

Getting up at 4am, not being allowed to sit outside, strict clothing rules, rules designed for other residents and applied unequally. With nothing to do, conflict follows. As the facilitator summarised it: freedom is not only leaving the centre, but how girls can live inside it.

Different reasons, different needs

Migrant girls are in care because of the crisis at home, what they were drawn into, and arriving undocumented; local girls are in the centre for other reasons. Their needs are not the same and they should not be treated the same: they are not criminals. Neither group suffers more; both have unique needs and stories that deserve respect.

Fear of returning

Family rejection that started long before migration, losing their schooling, and the people who exploited them.

"Tú no sabes qué me ha pasado. Que yo esté tranquila no significa que no necesite apoyo (psicológico) como las demás."

You don't know what happened to me. Being calm doesn't mean I don't need (psychological) support like the others.

"Yo solo quiero salir a sentarme sola, mirar el cielo y decirle a las estrellas: estrella, me siento mal."

I just want to go out and sit alone, look at the sky and tell the stars: star, I feel bad.

"Si quieres corregirme está bien, pero no quieres corregirme, quieres humillarme."

If you want to correct me, that's fine, but you don't want to correct me, you want to humiliate me.

"Las personas malas conocen a mi familia y saben dónde vivimos… tengo miedo de que me secuestren otra vez si regreso."

The bad people know my family and where we live… I'm afraid they will kidnap me again if I go back.

What else helps: prayer, a Spanish-speaking staff member, visits from the Venezuelan community, and news about their case from the embassy and the Counter Trafficking Unit. Discrimination as Venezuelans shocked them, but among everything they face it is not their biggest worry.

Facilitators' reflection: the centre rescued and protects the girls, feeds them, lets them study and gives them a legal case and a pause to reflect, with limited funds. The girls want to move forward, but the system around them, in care and back home, does not yet help them get there.

conclusions

Until they are safe, not only better

The girls' journeys follow a cycle, like water:

  • Evaporation, before leaving: poverty, family breakdown and false promises push and pull girls to leave, often without knowing about trafficking, irregular status or even where they are going.
  • Condensation, exploitation and care: deception and exploitation, then protective care, where not knowing what will happen, little family contact, rationed mental health support and strict rules can also hurt.
  • Precipitation, after leaving: most say life is better, but better is not the same as safe. Many still face risks, and half were never followed up.
  • The sea, home or wherever they land: without support, the same conditions are waiting, and the cycle can start again.

Support at every stage breaks the cycle: the truth before girls leave, healing and dignity while they are in care, and follow-up that crosses the border with them.

Girls are not simply returning to the same conditions, but they are not leaving them behind either. Most former residents say life is better and that what they gained in care still helps, yet most still carry at least one vulnerability and half were never followed up. Girls still inside fear going back to family rejection, lost schooling and the people who exploited them.

Both groups agree on what hurts most in care: not knowing what will happen and being cut off from family. Girls still inside add what protection can do better: healing for every girl, adults who listen and keep confidences, rules that leave room to feel human, and education that gives them a way forward.

The cycle starts with not knowing and restarts when protection stops at the centre's door, or on a girl's 18th birthday. Ending violence against migrant children means telling them the truth before they leave, caring for their minds while they are in care, keeping families connected, and following girls across the border until they are safe, not only better.

  1. Marca si cada situación era parte de tu vida antes de llegar al centro, si es parte de tu vida ahora, o las dos. Responde solo lo que quieras. (Antes / Ahora / Prefiero no decirlo)
    • No tener dinero suficiente para lo básico (comida, alquiler)
    • Estar separada de mi familia o tener problemas en casa
    • No poder estudiar
    • No encontrar trabajo
    • No tener un lugar seguro donde vivir
    • Violencia, o sentirme insegura
    • Sentirme discriminada o excluida
    • No poder conseguir servicios (salud, apoyo, documentos)
  2. Comparada con ANTES de llegar al centro, tu vida ahora es: (Mejor / Más o menos igual / Más difícil / Prefiero no decirlo)
  3. ¿Sientes que las jóvenes que han salido del centro cuentan con suficiente apoyo para no volver a atravesar situaciones difíciles (como falta de vivienda, inseguridad o violencia)? (Sí, cuentan con apoyo suficiente / Tal vez, solo en algunos casos / No, siguen estando expuestas a riesgos / Prefiero no decirlo)
Lo que habría ayudado
  1. ¿Qué apoyo te habría ayudado más después de salir del centro? (abierta)
  2. Si pudieras hablar con los líderes y gobiernos, ¿qué les dirías para apoyar mejor a las chicas migrantes en centros residenciales? (abierta)
  3. ¿Hay algo más que te gustaría compartir sobre tu experiencia, opiniones, sugerencias? (abierta)
Sobre el formulario
  1. ¿Qué te pareció este formulario? (escala de 1 a 5)
  2. ¿Qué podemos mejorar en formularios como este? (abierta)
Annex B · Focus group guide for current residents

Planned for 75 minutes with 6–8 girls; held for 90 minutes with 6 girls. Questions talk about "las chicas" in general. ★ = priority questions. In practice, girls ranked each list instead of choosing three stickers.

1. Bienvenida, asentimiento y reglas · 5 min
2. Apertura · 5 min
  1. ¿Qué actividades disfrutan más las chicas aquí?
3. Lo que ayuda · 10 min

Votación 1 con stickers: lo que más ayuda (misma lista que la pregunta 4 de la encuesta).

  1. ★ Miren los stickers: ¿por qué creen que eso ayuda tanto?
  2. ★ ¿Qué hace que una persona que trabaja aquí sea de verdad un apoyo para las chicas? ¿Y qué hace que una chica se sienta juzgada o tratada como menos?
4. Lo difícil: espera, familia y llegada · 15 min

Votación 2 con stickers: lo más difícil (misma lista que la pregunta 6 de la encuesta).

  1. ★ ¿Cómo se enteran las chicas de lo que pasa con su caso? ¿Qué haría más fácil la espera?
  2. ★ Pensando en las llamadas y el contacto con la familia, ¿qué ayudaría de verdad? ¿Cada cuánto, por cuánto tiempo y de qué forma (voz, video, mensajes)? ¿Quién debería decidirlo?
  3. ★ ¿Qué ayudaría a una chica nueva en sus primeras semanas aquí?
  4. Si hay tiempo: ¿Qué ayudaría a las chicas a sentirse más conectadas con su cultura, su comida y su idioma?
5. Lo que dijeron las chicas que ya salieron · 10 min

Ronda de semáforo (verde: aquí también es así; amarillo: a veces; rojo: aquí no es así), una frase a la vez:

  • "Para muchas, lo más difícil fue no saber qué iba a pasar ni cuánto tiempo se quedarían."
  • "Las llamadas con la familia eran muy pocas y muy cortas, y eso les dolía mucho."
  • "El primer mes fue el más difícil: al llegar, una piensa y piensa demasiado."
  • "Lo que más les sirvió después de salir fue el inglés, los cursos y lo que aprendieron en psicología para manejar la ansiedad y tener paciencia."
  • "Al salir, a muchas les preocupaba sufrir discriminación o tener que migrar otra vez."
  1. ★ Si una chica que no la pasó bien aquí estuviera con nosotras, ¿qué creen que diría?
6. Mirando hacia adelante · 15 min

Votación 3 con stickers: lo que más preocupa al salir (misma lista que la pregunta 11 de la encuesta).

  1. ★ Cuando las chicas piensan en salir, ¿qué es lo que más les preocupa del lugar al que van a volver?
  2. ★ Algunas chicas salen porque cumplen 18 años. ¿Qué necesita saber o tener una chica antes de salir?
  3. ★ ¿Qué necesitan entender las chicas sobre sus documentos y su situación legal antes de salir? ¿Quién se lo explica ahora?
  4. ★ ¿Qué apoyo haría la diferencia en los primeros meses fuera? ¿Quién debería darlo?
  5. Si hay tiempo: ¿Cuáles son tus sueños para el futuro, y qué apoyo ayudaría a las chicas a lograrlos?
7. Mensaje a los líderes · 10 min
  1. ★ Si líderes de distintos países te estuvieran escuchando hoy, ¿qué te gustaría que supieran? (Tarjeta anónima, escrita o dibujada, con casilla de permiso para mostrarla en la conferencia.)
8. Cierre · 5 min
  1. ¿Hay algo importante de lo que no hemos hablado hoy?
